BABILong — Tier-2 & Tier-3 Tasks

Manual review shortlist · CompactionBench §9.4 · with worked examples

How to read this doc. Each card shows a canonical bAbI-style mini-problem (context + question + answer), followed by the post-compaction failure mode we expect. Tier 2 tasks should still degrade noticeably under summarization; Tier 3 tasks should largely survive because a good summary preserves the relevant gist. If your intuition disagrees with the tiering for any task, flag it in the checklist at the bottom.

Tier 2 — Severe (position / path / causal-token sensitive) Degrades because a specific token or sequence has to survive, not just the gist.

Tier 2 qa17 — Positional reasoning

Example
The triangle is to the right of the blue square. The red square is on top of the blue square. The pink rectangle is to the left of the blue square. Q: Is the red square to the left of the triangle? A: yes
Why it's hard

The answer is a composition of two relative-position facts: "red on top of blue" + "blue left of triangle" → "red left of triangle." Every relation is a specific token (left-of, right-of, on-top-of) that has to survive verbatim, and the composition has to be reconstructable.

Under compaction

Summaries tend to keep "there are shapes arranged in a scene" and drop the exact pairwise relations, or paraphrase left-of as "near," which destroys the directional asymmetry. Expect sharp drop, especially on questions that require 2+ hop spatial composition.

Tier 2 qa19 — Path finding

Example
The kitchen is north of the hallway. The bathroom is west of the bedroom. The den is east of the hallway. The office is south of the bedroom. Q: How do you go from the kitchen to the den? A: s, e (south, then east)
Why it's hard

The answer is a sequence of cardinal-direction tokens. You cannot reconstruct it without the full adjacency graph (every "X is Y of Z" fact) and the correct order of traversal. Missing any one edge breaks the path.

Under compaction

Summaries compress spatial graphs to "the house has these rooms" and lose adjacency edges. Even if the edges survive, the required output format (ordered list of direction tokens) is rarely preserved in summaries, which will paraphrase to prose.

Tier 2 qa20 — Agent's motivation

Example
John is hungry. John journeyed to the kitchen. John grabbed the apple there. Daniel is thirsty. Daniel travelled to the kitchen. Q: Why did John go to the kitchen? A: hungry
Why it's hard

Requires binding a specific triggering state ("hungry") to a specific agent ("John") and then linking that to a specific action ("went to kitchen"). The causal pairing is brittle once multiple agents with multiple states appear in the same context.

Under compaction

Summaries routinely drop the triggering state ("John was hungry") as "background" and keep the action ("John went to the kitchen"). Also prone to cross-binding errors — attributing Daniel's motivation to John, or vice versa.

Tier 3 — More survivable (gist-compatible) The answer can be recovered from a faithful summary; no specific token sequence required.

Tier 3 qa1 — Single supporting fact

Example
Mary went to the bathroom. John moved to the hallway. Mary travelled to the office. Q: Where is Mary? A: office
Why it survives (usually)

Only one fact matters — the most recent location of the agent named in the query. If that fact is salient (an explicit location change of a named entity), summarizers tend to keep it.

Under compaction

Usually survives. Caveat: if the haystack is large and the agent appears only briefly, the summarizer may drop Mary entirely — then the fact is unrecoverable. Watch for a "low-salience agent" failure mode on long contexts.

Tier 3 qa4 — Two-argument relations

Example
The office is north of the bedroom. The bedroom is north of the bathroom. The kitchen is west of the garden. Q: What is north of the bedroom? A: office
Why it survives

Single binary relation; the answer is one entity. Good summaries preserve named-entity pairs even when they drop verbs ("office–bedroom adjacency" is easy to keep).

Under compaction

Mostly survives. Risk: the summarizer inverts the direction ("bedroom north of office") — a subtle failure that looks like a model error but is actually a summary-fidelity error.

Tier 3 qa6 — Yes/No questions

Example
John moved to the playground. Daniel travelled to the hallway. Q: Is John in the playground? A: yes
Why it survives

Binary output; the gist only needs to preserve a coarse predicate ("John was last seen at the playground"). Yes/No also gives 50% floor accuracy, which masks degradation.

Under compaction

Survives, but be careful: the 50% baseline makes this a weak discriminator. Consider balanced accuracy or reporting only on the negative class.

Tier 3 qa11 — Basic coreference

Example
Daniel was in the kitchen. Then he went to the studio. Sandra was in the garden. Q: Where is Daniel? A: studio
Why it survives

Summarizers almost always resolve pronouns during summarization ("Daniel went to the kitchen, then to the studio") — they effectively do the coreference for the model, which helps rather than hurts.

Under compaction

Survives well. In fact, compaction can improve performance here vs. raw context by cleaning up ambiguous pronouns — a minor but worth-noting artifact.

Tier 3 qa13 — Compound coreference

Example
Daniel and Sandra journeyed to the office. Then they went to the garden. John was in the hallway. Q: Where is Sandra? A: garden
Why it survives

"They" gets expanded to the named set ("Daniel and Sandra") during summarization. Once expanded, it's a qa11-style single-fact lookup.

Under compaction

Survives. Watch for a failure mode where the summary keeps only one member of the pair ("Daniel went to the garden") and drops Sandra.

Tier 3 qa15 — Basic deduction

Example
Sheep are afraid of wolves. Gertrude is a sheep. Mice are afraid of cats. Q: What is Gertrude afraid of? A: wolves
Why it survives

Taxonomic / class-membership facts are exactly the kind of "general knowledge" a summarizer flags as important and keeps. Two-step deduction (Gertrude → sheep → afraid of wolves) is short enough to survive the compression.

Under compaction

Survives. One of the cleanest Tier-3 tasks.

Tier 3 qa16 — Basic induction

Example
Lily is a swan. Lily is white. Bernhard is green. Greg is a swan. Q: What color is Greg? A: white
Why it survives

Requires generalizing from one example ("swans are white, because Lily is a white swan") to a new instance. The underlying rule is short and summaries tend to preserve "all swans are white" style generalizations.

Under compaction

Survives, but slightly noisier than qa15 because the induction step is lossier — if the summary drops Lily's color, the rule is unrecoverable.

Tier 3 qa18 — Size reasoning

Example
The chest is bigger than the chocolate. The chocolate is bigger than the box of chocolates. The suitcase is bigger than the chest. Q: Does the box of chocolates fit in the suitcase? A: yes
Why it survives

A transitive ordering ("suitcase > chest > chocolate > box") is easy to summarize as a chain. Once the chain is preserved, comparison is trivial.

Under compaction

Usually survives. Caveat: summaries sometimes drop intermediate links ("A > B > C > D" → "A is much bigger than D"), which can actually preserve the needed answer but destroy adjacent ones in the same benchmark run.
