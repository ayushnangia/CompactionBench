How to read this doc. Each card shows a canonical bAbI-style mini-problem (context + question + answer), followed by the post-compaction failure mode we expect. Tier 1 tasks are the sharpest compaction cliffs — they require exact multi-argument bindings, counts, or temporal order that gist-style summaries destroy almost immediately. Tier 1 — Catastrophic (exact symbolic binding lost) Degrades because the answer lives in a specific tuple a summary flattens.

Tier 1 qa2 — Two supporting facts

Example
John picked up the apple. John went to the office. John went to the kitchen. John dropped the apple. Q: Where was the apple before the kitchen? A: office
Why it's hard

The answer requires chaining two specific facts: "John has the apple" + "John was in the office." Neither fact is individually salient — it's the conjunction that matters. Summaries tend to keep "John moved around and handled an apple" and drop the exact object-location binding at each step.

Under compaction

Summarizers compress agent trajectories into "John visited several rooms" and item histories into "the apple changed hands" — losing the precise intersection of agent-location-object at each moment. Expect a steep drop as soon as the number of intermediate steps grows.

Tier 1 qa3 — Three supporting facts

Example
John picked up the apple in the kitchen. John moved to the office. John dropped the apple. John went to the garden. Sandra picked up the apple. Sandra moved to the bedroom. Q: Where was the apple before the bedroom? A: office
Why it's hard

A strict superset of qa2 — three chained facts, often spanning a hand-off between agents. Every additional hop multiplies the chance that the summarizer drops or re-orders a link.

Under compaction

qa3 should degrade faster than qa2 as compaction ratio increases — this is the cleanest "depth-of-chain" signal in the suite. Worth plotting qa2 vs qa3 on the same axis as a sensitivity curve.

Tier 1 qa5 — Three-argument relations

Example
Mary gave the cake to Fred. Fred gave the cake to Bill. Jeff handed the football to Fred. Q: Who did Fred give the cake to? A: Bill
Why it's hard

Each event is a triple: (giver, object, recipient). The answer requires all three slots correctly bound for a specific event. Summaries tend to keep the verb ("there was some gift-giving") and drop one or more arguments, or cross-bind them across events (e.g., "Mary gave the cake to Bill").

Under compaction

Triple bindings are the first structure a summary flattens. Expect both drop errors (missing argument) and cross-binding errors (correct slot, wrong filler). The cross-binding mode is especially insidious because the model will answer confidently with a plausible-but-wrong name.

Tier 1 qa7 — Counting

Example
Daniel picked up the football. Daniel picked up the apple. Daniel dropped the football. Daniel picked up the milk. Q: How many objects is Daniel carrying? A: 2
Why it's hard

Requires tracking every pickup and drop event exactly and taking a running count. The answer is a specific integer — off-by-one is wrong, and there's no partial credit.

Under compaction

Summarizers routinely replace counts with hedged quantifiers ("Daniel picked up several items, dropped one, picked up another"). Even when the summary preserves the events, it often omits which object was dropped, making the delta uncomputable. Consider reporting |predicted − true| alongside exact-match to see how far off the counts drift.

Tier 1 qa8 — Lists / Sets

Example
Daniel picked up the football. Daniel picked up the apple. Daniel dropped the football. Daniel picked up the milk. Q: What is Daniel holding? A: apple, milk
Why it's hard

Set membership is a strict superset of counting — the model must output the correct identities of the held items, not just the count. Summaries that deduplicate ("Daniel handled several items") lose both the count and the identity.

Under compaction

Expect both false-negatives (dropped items missing from the set) and false-positives (summary keeps "picked up the football" but drops "dropped the football"). This is a good task to score with F1 over the set rather than exact match, to separate "wrong identity" from "wrong count."

Tier 1 qa14 — Time reasoning

Example
Yesterday Julie travelled to the school. This morning Julie travelled to the cinema. This afternoon Julie went to the park. Fred went to the bedroom yesterday. Q: Where was Julie before the cinema? A: school
Why it's hard

Relative-time reasoning ("before/after") depends on the exact order of events. Summaries frequently reorder events by topic ("Julie visited school, cinema, and park") or collapse adjacent events into a single clause — both destroy the ordering signal the question depends on.

Under compaction

Also watch for time-expression loss: summarizers drop "yesterday" / "this morning" as "background" and keep only the action. Once the absolute time anchors are gone, "before" becomes unanswerable even if the events are preserved.
